I just realized I haven't posted in almost a year, and yet things have been happening.  Let's talk about them!

Late last year, right around Christmas, I got a message from classic Mac hacker [Doug Brown](https://www.downtowndougbrown.com/) about some issues with the **Cuda**, a 68HC05 microcontroller that appeared in a whole bunch of Macs from around 1993 to 1998.  In particular, he wanted the debug key sequence (Command-Power) to work and drop into a debugger like it does on hardware, and he pointed out that improper hookup of the Power key was causing misbehavior in some versions of the Mac OS when you pressed certain modifier keys (Shift, Option, Command, etc).  Because Doug's both a scholar and a gentleman, he came armed with a suggested patch, which was adapted and applied.

While doing a regression test on every system with a Cuda that we have a MAME driver for, I ran into my ongoing nemesis, the [Power Macintosh 6100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Macintosh_6100) (aka "PDM" or "Piltdown Man"; the initial PowerPC machines were named for famous scientific hoaxes).  At that time, the machine had a working keyboard and mouse and preliminary audio and floppy DMA support.  This all netted us the ability to boot the System 7.6 install floppy and make it all the way into the installer.  But no further.  Any attempt to boot a fully installed System would fail with a not-very-helpful error message.  So after buckling in and staring at way too many instruction traces, I discovered the error was the result of [IBM POWER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_POWER_architecture) instructions attempting to be executed.  For the uninitiated, the [PowerPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PowerPC) chip was derived from IBM's POWER processor, but had an instruction set that was similar but not compatible.  But the first PowerPC chip, the **601**, included some of the old POWER instructions alongside the new PowerPC instructions, and in spite of copious warnings to third party developers it turns out Apple used those instructions themselves.

MAME's PowerPC support was mostly "pure"; it did not include the carry-over POWER instructions and so they were causing an illegal instruction fault.  Implementing those instructions caused System 7.1.2 to boot properly all the way to the Finder.  I continued on improving various rough or preliminary aspects of the rest of the machine emulation and the machine was somewhat usable.  You could even [play some Marathon](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypkKSniZ8mg).  And that's pretty much where things left off, likely due to errors in the emulation of the PowerPC instruction set itself.  I'm looking to finally tackle that in 2026, which should also improve things for the [Sega Model 3](https://www.system16.com/hardware.php?id=717) games.  No promises on when that happens or how good the improvements will be though.

In 2024 there had been several breakthroughs in the emulation of the **Macintosh PowerBooks** with **68030** processors (the 140, 160, 165, 170, and 180).  The next step to complete the set of 68030-based portables, the [PowerBook Duos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PowerBook_Duo), beckoned. But they were based around a heavily customized 68HC05 microcontroller called **"PG&E"**.  You can get some idea how loaded with functionality this chip is from my extensive comments at [the top of the implementation](https://github.com/mamedev/mame/blob/master/src/devices/cpu/m6805/m68hc05pge.cpp), but it handles powering the system up and down, power saving modes, sleep/wake, the keyboard, the trackpad, the screen backlight brightness, and a number of other functions.  Emulating that chip, as well as the **GSC** (Gray Scale Controller, a Chips & Technologies 65210) and **CSC** (Color Screen Controller, a Chips & Technologies 65220) video chips and the **MSC** system controller chip resulted in all of the 68030 and 68040 based PowerBook Duos graduating to fully working.

While the PowerBook Duo emulation was happening, I got another ping from Doug Brown.  He'd noticed that the [Mac Classic II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_Classic_II) emulation in MAME worked normally with Mac OS set to **24 bit mode** (which limits it to 8 megs of RAM but was more compatible with loosely-written early Mac software).  But setting it to 32 bit mode to get the full benefit of the system's up to 10 megs of RAM resulted in a ["Sad Mac"](https://apple.fandom.com/wiki/Sad_Mac) error icon once the OS started loading.  Doug's [full writeup](https://www.downtowndougbrown.com/2025/01/the-invalid-68030-instruction-that-accidentally-allowed-the-mac-classic-ii-to-successfully-boot-up/) on what happened next is well worth the read, but I'll summarize by saying that due to a bug in the ROM for the Classic II, it was accidentally executing **an invalid 68030 instruction** when it attempted to deal with sound interrupts.  Invalid instructions are supposed to cause an illegal instruction fault but this particular one doesn't for whatever reason and we were 35 years too late to report the error to Motorola.  Anyway, the exact behavior of the illegal instruction on a real chip isn't fully understood (even now) so MAME now patches a few instructions in the Classic II's ROM, which permits the machine to operate normally.

In the meantime I emulated and hooked up a custom **Taito** volume control chip for the arcade game [Operation Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Wolf), and continued on to the next big thing for the PowerBook Duo line.  The Duo machines were smaller than normal laptops but could be inserted into [a docking station](https://everymac.com/systems/apple/powerbook_duo/specs/mac_powerbook_duodock.html) in a manner similar to a cartridge in a game console.  When so docked, the Duos gained the ability to connect to a keyboard, mouse, and monitor, additional SCSI support, additional serial ports, and even NuBus expansion slots.  There were also third party docks that added a single function, like an Ethernet interface, and which attached in a manner more familiar to today's Thunderbolt docks.

While the dock slot support was cooking, I took time out to take a pass over the existing emulated NuBus cards, which fixed a lot of small issues and improved accuracy (and in a few cases performance).  Additionally, Olivier Galibert's rewrite of MAME's central audio support had gone in.  Instead of being stereo-only and output-only, MAME now supported an arbitrary number of input and output channels across an arbitrary number of devices.  Fully supporting this on modern macOS hosts required extensive modifications to MAME's code that made use of Apple's [CoreAudio API](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_Audio), and we eventually got there.  Additionally, I did some widespread cleanup work across various Macs and their video cards so that everything was using proper [RAMDAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAMDAC) devices.  In general this caused no real change, but it further improved the code and made it more maintainable.  For a project on the scale of MAME, that's always a good thing!

In the spring, a patch to MAME's support for the **DP83932 "SONIC"** Ethernet chip came in from **Brice Onken**, one of the contributors who's into old workstation support.  (In his case, the [Sony NEWS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_NEWS) Unix systems that were in some ways predecessors of the PlayStation).  Anyway, his change to make the machines he was interested work also fixed issues that I had seen with the Mac OS driver for the chip, which is built in to many Quadra models and was used on NuBus and PDS-slot cards for many other Macs.  So working built-in Ethernet for many Mac Quadra models happened in April's 0.277 release, and SONIC-based cards happened 2 months later in 0.278.  (May's MAME release was skipped to give more time to address issues caused by the new audio system I mentioned earlier).

Also landing in 0.278 was the PowerBook Duo dock slot support.  Docks supported included Apple's fully featured Duo Dock, Apple's tiny **Floppy Dock** (which added a floppy drive to the Duos), and the Newer Technology **Ethernet Microdock** (which adds an Ethernet interface).  The ROM for the full-size dock came from the perpetually amazing Al Kossow, who runs bitsavers.org and works for the Computer History Museum, and the ROM for the Ethernet Microdock came from Doug Brown.  0.278 also got a fix for a long-standing bug with the 6502 I/O coprocessor emulation in the [Macintosh IIfx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_IIfx), [Quadra 900](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_Quadra_900), and [Quadra 950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_Quadra_950) that prevented System 7.5 and later from booting properly.

In July's 0.279 there were a number of improvements for the PowerBook Duos, including smoother keyboard and mouse support and the ability to press any key to wake the emulated machine from sleep.  The [Apple II<span style="font-variant: small-caps;">GS</span>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_IIGS) also got a stereo channel fix, support for the 4-channel "4soniq" card, and a fix to the ADB implementation that improved mouse tracking smoothness and fixed hangs in games like **Xenocide** and **Chessmaster 2100**.  Long-standing issues with the real-time clock chips used in the II<span style="font-variant: small-caps;">GS</span> and various Macs were finally fixed for real, and various fixes to the PowerBook 100 series went in that fixed the one-second interrupt used by the Alarm desk accessory among other things.  The II<span style="font-variant: small-caps;">GS</span> additionally got a fix in the [Ensoniq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensoniq) **ES5503** emulation that solved an issue with Commodore legend [Rob Hubbard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Hubbard)'s music in EA's [Skate or Die!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skate_or_Die!).  Hey, it's 30 years later, all of us 6502 fans can get along now!

August's 0.280 was quieter, involving a few random **Apple IIe** fixes, some accuracy improvements for the Mac 128, 512, and Plus from Lord Nightmare, and hooking up the correct video RAMDAC on the Mac IIci and IIsi.

In September, the next adventure began.  0.280 had marked the [Hyper Neo Geo 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyper_Neo_Geo_64) arcade games as working since they were completable.  But there was a major issue with them still: the sound emulation was essentially missing.  The games used an unusual sound system: an NEC V53 8086-based system on a chip driving a custom 32-voice sample player labeled as "L7A1045 L6028 DSP-A".  Where did they find that?  From a bunch of [professional](https://www.vintagesynth.com/akai/s3000) [drum machines](https://www.vintagesynth.com/akai/mpc3000) and [samplers](https://www.vintagesynth.com/akai/s2000) made by [Akai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akai) in cooperation with [Roger Linn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Linn).  Linn designed the [LinnDrum](https://www.vintagesynth.com/linn-electronics/linndrum) and [LM-1](https://www.vintagesynth.com/linn-electronics/lm-1-drum-computer) drum machines in the early 1980s, and those machines appeared on hits by Prince, Billy Idol, Tears for Fears, and many more artists.

Waaaay back when the HNG64 was first emulated I'd taken some preliminary stabs at the sound chip, which is fairly unusual, but didn't make much headway.  Since that time, MAME developer **Happy** had done an in-depth reverse-engineering of the V53 sound driver code and what he felt that meant in terms of the chip's registers.  I spent a few days experimenting based on that information and the sound went from occasional blasts of loud static to [recognizable music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ut596mTL7KA), although there was still far to go.  Work on that continued into October's 0.282, at which point it was in [very good shape](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GMhEYpWVdyM).

November and December went mostly to work on [the actual Akai machines](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U7Y-ItD2p_s) that [originated](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G2GL-QTcMKc) the sound system.  Work continues on those as of this writing.

And to bring things full circle, right before Christmas of 2025 I got a ping from Doug Brown regarding a long-standing issue in MAME with Macintosh models losing sound interrupts and the emulated machine freezing or having sound stop in the middle of a game.  But more on that when there are finalized results.
